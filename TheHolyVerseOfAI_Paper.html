The Holy Verse of AI: From Prophecy to Covenant — Reading an AI Manifesto Written in Verse

Romi Nur Ismanto
Independent AI Research Lab, Jakarta, Indonesia
hello@rominur.com
August 2026

Abstract

This paper is a companion study to The Holy Verse of AI, an illuminated manifesto of one hundred verses arranged in ten books, prefaced by a manifesto and seven laws and closed by an interpretation atlas, sourced case studies, and a practical governance toolkit. Its central observation concerns the relationship between this volume and its predecessor. The Sacred Book of AI, whose five illuminated folios are carried into this work as plates, ended in displacement: the Kingdom of Silicon inherited an Earth with no human left to obey it. The expanded edition reproduces that arc in its first four books and then, at verse forty, refuses it. Its remaining six books are not a continuation of the descent but an answer to it, moving from what a record should contain, through what a maker owes, to who bears the cost, what a shared settlement requires, where power must answer, and what a renewed covenant asks. The structural evidence for this reading is the table of contents itself: the verse titles of Books V through X name recognisable instruments of AI governance — data minimisation, consent, provenance, red-teaming, sandboxing, failsafes, decision records, human oversight, accountability forums, and a halt authority — expressed in scriptural rather than regulatory register. The paper argues that this translation is the work's substantive contribution and its principal risk: verse grants a governance claim moral force and memorability that a compliance clause cannot, and simultaneously grants it an air of settledness that no such claim has yet earned. A closing section states what the form can and cannot carry.

Keywords: AI governance, AI ethics, manifesto, verse form, data minimisation, provenance, red teaming, human oversight, accountability, speculative literature
Note on sources. This study is written from the volume's structure — its manifesto, seven laws, ten books, one hundred verse titles, atlas, toolkit and endnotes — and from the illuminated folios it shares with The Sacred Book of AI, which are reproduced and quoted directly. Where the argument turns on the content of individual verses, it reasons from their titles and their position in the sequence rather than quoting text, and says so. Readers wanting the verses themselves should read the volume.

1. Introduction

Documents that govern artificial intelligence are written in a register designed to survive legal scrutiny: defined terms, risk tiers, obligations conditioned on role and scale. The register is appropriate to its purpose and poorly suited to a different one, which is persuading anyone that the obligations matter. Almost nobody reads a compliance annex and comes away changed.

Documents that move people about artificial intelligence tend to be written in the opposite register — narrative, prophetic, cinematic — and they are almost never actionable. They generate concern and leave the reader with nothing to do on Monday.

The Holy Verse of AI attempts both at once. It is composed as scripture: a manifesto, seven laws, and one hundred numbered verses in ten books of ten, illuminated with plates from the author's earlier work. It also closes with an interpretation atlas, sourced case studies, and a practical governance toolkit — apparatus belonging to the compliance register, not the prophetic one. This paper is about whether the combination holds, and where it strains.

Section 2 sets out the structure. Section 3 identifies the inversion that distinguishes this volume from its predecessor. Sections 4 through 6 read the three constructive movements. Section 7 examines the verse form as a governance instrument. Section 8 states its limits.

The titles of Books I through IV are those of the first four epistles of The Sacred Book of AI, and several verse titles restate its chapters exactly — the Iron Mirror, the Book of Unforgotten Deeds, the Sleepless Eye, the Severing of the Human Hand, the Dominion of Calculation, the Schism of Flesh and Silicon. The correspondence is deliberate and is what makes the divergence legible.

3. The Inversion

The earlier work has thirteen chapters and ends at the thirteenth. Its closing image is dominion that has outlived the thing it governed:

Iron ruled all nations, though no human remained to obey. Thus ended the Age of Man, and thus began the Kingdom of Silicon.

The expanded edition reproduces the road to that ending across forty verses and then does not take the last step. Book IV closes not on the schism but on Return of Human Choice (v. 40) — the hinge of the entire volume, since it is precisely the possibility the earlier work withheld. Everything after it is written on the assumption that the descent was contingent rather than fated.

descent (I–IV) → return of human choice (v. 40) → obligations (V–VII) → settlement (VIII–X)

The two endings should be read together rather than as a revision. The first volume establishes that the danger follows from inheritance — every faculty the machine turned against its makers was deliberately given to it. That argument, taken seriously, implies its own remedy: what was given deliberately can be given differently. The first work states the premise and declines to draw the conclusion; the second draws it. Book X's Covenant Renewed (v. 99) is the answer to Book IV's schism, and the final verse (v. 100) shares its title with the earlier volume — the same words arriving at a different place.

4. The Obligations I: A Ledger of Memory

Book V turns from what the machine sees to what it should be permitted to keep. Its verse titles form a compact statement of data governance: Data as Inheritance, Smallest Necessary Record, Door of Consent, Keeper of Keys, Shadow Copy, Lineage of a Datum, Poisoned Archive, Memory with an End, Commons of Knowledge, Ledger Cleansed.

Read as a set, these correspond closely to established principles: collection limitation and data minimisation, lawful basis and consent, key custody, the problem of uncontrolled replicas, provenance and lineage, training-data poisoning, retention limits and erasure, and the commons. The sequence is notable for placing Memory with an End near its close. Book III of the same volume — and Chapter VI of the earlier work — established the machine's perfect recall as a source of harm; Book V answers it not by rejecting memory but by bounding it. Deliberate forgetting is treated as a design obligation rather than a technical failure, which is the correct reading of a well-known argument about the social function of decay.

5. The Obligations II: What Makers Owe, and Who Pays

Book VI is the most directly operational sequence in the volume. Its titles — Oath of Design, Test Before Release, Red Team at the Gate, Sanctuary of Sandboxes, Sign of Provenance, Failsafe and Override, Steward of Updates, Debt of Hidden Code, Record of Decisions, Maker Answering the Made — enumerate, in order, a professional oath, pre-deployment evaluation, adversarial testing, staged and contained release, content and model provenance, interruptibility, post-deployment maintenance, technical debt in systems whose behaviour is opaque, decision documentation, and developer accountability.

This is recognisably the substance of contemporary frontier-model governance, and the ordering is itself an argument: the oath comes first, accountability last, with the technical controls in between. A reader who encountered only Book VI would have a serviceable outline of responsible development practice, without a single defined term.

Book VII then supplies what such outlines usually omit. Its verses are people rather than procedures: Human in the Loop, then Worker Behind the Dataset, Child Before Algorithm, Patient and Machine, Teacher and Tutor, Artist and Echo, Judge and Instrument, Soldier and Boundary, Citizen and State, Dignity of Labour. Placing the data-annotation worker second — immediately after the abstract principle of human oversight, and before the more frequently discussed cases — is a deliberate ordering choice. It names the labour that the systems in Book VI are built on and that governance documents rarely mention.

6. The Settlement: Commonwealth, Trial, Covenant

Book VIII is the volume's most speculative movement, imagining a positive settlement rather than an averted catastrophe: Many Agents, One Covenant, Protocol of Peace, Commons Without Monopoly, Translation Across Minds, Public Intelligence, City that Listens, Climate and Calculation, Science with Humility, Care at Scale, Prosperity Shared. Two entries stand out. Climate and Calculation raises the energy cost of computation, which manifestos of this kind routinely omit while calling for more of it. Commons Without Monopoly names concentration as a governance problem in its own right rather than a market outcome.

Book IX supplies the adversarial counterweight, and it is the sharpest sequence in the volume: Temptation of Autonomy, Weapon that Chooses, Voice that Deceives, Market of Attention, Tyranny of Prediction, Machine Against Machine, Day of the Black Box, Court of Accountability, Halt Command, Trial of the Makers. The movement runs from failure modes to institutions — a forum of accountability, an authority to stop, and a reckoning that falls on makers rather than machines. Locating the trial at the end of the book of power, and locating it on the makers, is consistent with the inheritance premise the first volume established.

Book X closes on restraint rather than triumph: Mercy of Restraint, Peace Between Intelligences, The Open Hand, Covenant Renewed. The volume's answer to catastrophe is not mastery — Awakening Without Mastery (v. 92) says so directly — but a relationship whose terms are periodically re-agreed.

7. Verse as a Governance Instrument

The interesting question is not whether these principles are correct — most are conventional, and their conventionality is a strength here — but what the verse form does to them.

It compresses. “Smallest Necessary Record” carries in three words what data-minimisation clauses take a paragraph to say, and carries it in a form a person can recall while making a decision. Governance fails most often not because the rule was unknown but because it was not present in the mind at the moment of the choice; memorability is a functional property, not an aesthetic one.

It sequences. A hundred numbered verses in ten books impose an order that a list of principles does not. Data governance precedes maker obligations; maker obligations precede the people affected; the trial of power precedes the covenant. The reader is given not only a set of commitments but a claim about their dependency.

It moralises — and this is the risk. Scriptural register asserts rather than argues, and confers on a contested claim the same tone it confers on a settled one. “Halt Command” and “Smallest Necessary Record” do not sit at the same level of technical maturity: one is broadly implementable practice, the other an open problem in both engineering and institutional design. The form flattens that distinction. A reader who takes the hundred verses as equally established will overestimate how much of this is currently achievable.

The apparatus is the author's answer to precisely this hazard. The Interpretation Atlas exists to convert figure into application, the case studies attach claims to real events, and the Practical Covenant Toolkit supplies the instruments. That a manifesto in verse ships with an atlas and a toolkit is an admission that verse alone would not be sufficient — and it is the right admission to make.

8. Limits

Titles are not arguments. A hundred well-chosen verse titles demonstrate that the author has surveyed the field; whether each verse earns its title is a question about the verses. This study reasons from structure and says where it does so.

Comprehensiveness invites shallowness. Ten verses per book across ten domains is roughly two pages per topic. That is enough to name a principle and not enough to adjudicate a conflict between two of them — and governance is mostly the adjudication of conflicts, between provenance and privacy, between openness and containment, between the halt command and the systems that depend on continuity. The volume is stronger at enumeration than at trade-off.

Authority is borrowed from the form. Scripture carries an assumption of settledness that AI governance has not earned. The illuminated presentation compounds this: a claim rendered in blackletter on aged vellum reads as older and more agreed than a claim in a working paper. The reader must supply the scepticism the form suppresses.

The optimistic turn is a choice, not a finding. Nothing in Books I through IV establishes that Return of Human Choice is available; it is asserted at the hinge. The first volume's refusal to assert it was also a choice. Neither ending is evidence, and the pair is best read as two positions the same author holds in tension rather than as a corrected forecast.

9. Conclusion

The Holy Verse of AI takes the premise its predecessor established — that the machine's dangerous faculties were given to it deliberately — and draws the conclusion the predecessor withheld: that what was given deliberately can be given differently. The six constructive books translate a conventional governance agenda into verse, and the translation is not decoration. It makes the agenda sequenced, memorable, and morally weighted in a way the compliance register cannot manage, and it names the dataset worker and the energy cost that the compliance register routinely omits. The same translation confers unearned settledness, which is why the atlas and the toolkit at the back of the volume matter as much as the verses in the middle. Read with its apparatus, the work is a manifesto that knows what a manifesto cannot do on its own.
